\section{Introduction}
Robotic surgery has become progressively more important in surgical procedures, with systems like the da Vinci surgical robot being utilised in hospitals across the world. However, such systems are currently fully tele-operated; that is, the surgeon must maintain continuous spatial awareness of the operating environment through a narrow endoscopic view. As the medical field transitions towards higher levels of robotic autonomy, machines will require a real-time understanding of the surgical scene: Where the camera is, what structures are present and how they deform under physiological motion and interaction. 

Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping (SLAM) addresses the joint problem of mapping an unknown environment and localising the robot within it. Robotic systems inherently require two things for positioning: a map of where they are, and their relative location inside that map. This creates a chicken-and-egg problem, as mapping and localisation are mutually dependent. Modern dense neural SLAM systems — iMAP~\cite{Sucar2021}, NICE-SLAM~\cite{Zhu2022}, and Co-SLAM~\cite{Wang2023} — achieve accurate real-time tracking and reconstruction, but all assume a static world and optimise the camera pose directly against the map’s own predictions. 

In surgery, the static world assumption  breaks; as the map itself is dynamic, camera pose and mapping can no longer be updated on the direct assumption that the scene is able to be trusted. For example in ORB-SLAM - pose updates rely on ORB-features being tracked between each frame; in dynamic scenes this is no longer possible - As the scene itself is moving, you can't trust it in pose updates. In addition to this, existing surgical SLAM datasets are limited, Semantic-SuPer~\cite{Lin2023} does not provide ground truth camera poses, and StereoMiS~\cite{Hayoz2023} only segments tools from the remainder of the scene (I.e., no dense tissue segmentation).

Given the aforementioned, this project proposes the application of semantic SLAM for surgical scenes, utilising the Comprehensive Robotic Cholecystectomy Dataset (CRCD)\cite{Oh2024}. The dataset provides endoscopic video with synchronised robot kinematics across 18 ex vivo procedures. Our project augments the dataset with the additional modalities required for a semantic SLAM pipeline, including the re-propagation of masks using the latest segmentation models. In addition to depth maps, created using MoGe2 and Stereo-matched to ensure accurate scaling. Finally, the necessary pose data is extracted and cleaned in Section~\ref{sec:motion_analysis}. From there detailed data sequences are constructed which contain all the required files for semantic SLAM - Data is provided in a TUM format with Semantics being in the same format as the Replica dataset, for easy reusability with other models. 

This augmented dataset is benchmarked on multiple state-of-the-art (SoTA) semantic SLAM pipelines, and proves challenging for most of them. In parallel with the dataset, we present \textbf{BDDS-SLAM}, an uncertainty-aware extension of DDS-SLAM~\cite{Shan2024} that improves camera tracking and reconstruction under scene deformation through three contributions: a learned aleatoric uncertainty head that down-weights unreliable measurements in both tracking and mapping; a label-free motion attribution built on DINO features that isolates camera motion from scene motion and freezes the pose on confident-still frames; and the diagnosis and correction of an optimiser-induced pose noise floor in the bundle adjustment. Every addition is flag-gated, so the released baseline is reproduced exactly and each measured gain is attributable to a single change. Calibrated on two design sequences and tested unseen on the remaining three, BDDS-SLAM improves trajectory error on all five ($-31\%$ on average, up to $-51\%$ held-out) while preserving rendering quality, and records the best trajectory of every SoTA system we benchmark.



\section{Contributions}
This thesis provides 3 notable contributions to the field:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A one-shot, human-in-the-loop segmentation strategy for unseen surgical domains built on the frozen foundation model SAM~3 \cite{Carion2025}: sparse human-annotated anchor keyframes seed SAM~3's video propagation to generate the dense per-frame semantic annotations a semantic SLAM pipeline requires, without fine-tuning the model.
\item A reorganisation of the CRCD into self-contained \texttt{Sequences} consumable by both TUM and Replica data-loaders (Section~\ref{sec:data_packs}), establishing a benchmark for semantic SLAM in surgical environments.
\item \textbf{BDDS-SLAM}, an uncertainty-aware extension of DDS-SLAM~\cite{Shan2024} on a neural-SDF substrate, contributing: (i) aleatoric-uncertainty down-weighting, ported from WildGS-SLAM~\cite{Zheng2024} to a surgical neural-SDF system via a small uncertainty head (UncertaintyNet) that drives both tracking and mapping; (ii) a label-free split of camera ego-motion from independent scene motion, combining frozen self-supervised vision-transformer features~\cite{Oquab2023,Simeoni2025} with a depth-signature egomotion fit and a geometric epipolar agreement, with no trained segmenter --- consumed as a pose freeze that is re-pinned through bundle adjustment so the decision persists in the trajectory; and (iii) the diagnosis and correction of an optimiser-induced pose noise floor in the bundle adjustment, in which the adaptive optimiser's gradient-magnitude-invariant step walks every keyframe by a fixed distance per cycle regardless of the residual --- predicted from the update rule, measured at its predicted magnitude in every configuration including the unmodified baseline, and removed at its predicted magnitude by the correction. Locked on two design sequences and run unseen on three held-out sequences, the combination improves tracking on all five ($-31\%$ mean ATE against the unmodified base, $-51\%$ on the largest-travel held-out sequence) at level rendering quality.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Related Works}
\paragraph{Classical and deformable SLAM.}
ORB-SLAM3~\cite{Campos2021} is an example of classical feature-based SLAM (sparse SLAM), unifying multi-map data association across monocular, stereo, and RGB-D inputs. While  accurate, these indirect methods assume a rigid scene; an assumption violated by the movement of objects in a dynamic environment. DefSLAM~\cite{Lamarca2020} addressed this by introducing real-time monocular SLAM for deforming scenes, combining Shape-from-Template tracking with isometric Non-Rigid Structure-from-Motion for map updates. Its successor, SD-DefSLAM~\cite{Rodrguez2020}, improved robustness in surgical environments by fusing direct and indirect data association and using CNN-based semantic segmentation to mask surgical tools; demonstrating the value of semantic information for handling dynamic objects that would otherwise impact model tracking and mapping.

\paragraph{Neural-implicit SLAM.}
iMAP~\cite{Sucar2021} utilised a single MLP as a unified scene representation for real-time joint tracking and mapping; NICE-SLAM~\cite{Zhu2022} introduced hierarchical feature grids; and Co-SLAM~\cite{Wang2023} combined a multi-resolution hash grid with one-blob coordinate encodings, achieving robust camera tracking with smooth reconstruction. These systems optimise the camera pose against the photometric and geometric predictions of the map itself (frame-to-model tracking). All of these methods assume a static world, relying on trust in the map to make accurate pose updates.

\paragraph{Neural scene representations in surgery.}
The shift towards dense scene representations in surgery began in parallel with the above, with EndoNeRF~\cite{Wang2022}, which applied neural radiance fields with a canonical-plus-deformation framework to reconstruct deforming tissue under tool interaction. However, EndoNeRF and its successors assume known camera poses and do not address joint localisation and mapping. More recently, Gaussian-splatting representations have been adopted for surgical SLAM: EndoGSLAM~\cite{Wang2024} demonstrated dense reconstruction at over 100 frames per second using differentiable Gaussian rasterisation, though it operates under a rigid-scene assumption and requires RGB-D input.

\paragraph{Robustness to dynamic content.}
Handling scene content that moves independently of the camera is a long-standing SLAM problem with two dominant strategies. Mask-based methods such as DynaSLAM~\cite{Bescos2018} detect movable objects with a trained segmenter and excise them from tracking. This is effective where reliable detectors exist, but depends on supervised class knowledge that is scarce in surgical domains. Uncertainty-based methods instead learn where the data is unreliable: WildGS-SLAM~\cite{Zheng2024} trains a shallow MLP on DINOv2 features~\cite{Oquab2023} to predict a per-pixel uncertainty that down-weights both tracking and mapping, removing transient distractors from casual in-the-wild video without explicit masks. The uncertainty-aware down-weighting developed in this thesis ports this second strategy, via the heteroscedastic loss of Kendall and Gal~\cite{KendallGal2017}, onto a neural-SDF surgical substrate, and its motion gating pursues the label-free goal by geometric rather than learned means.

\paragraph{Semantic surgical perception and DDS-SLAM.}
Semantic-SuPer~\cite{Lin2023} demonstrated that integrating semantics with geometric tracking improves data association and reconstruction in deforming endoscopic scenes. Building on this direction, DDS-SLAM~\cite{Shan2024} introduced a dense deformable semantic neural SLAM for endoscopy, extending the Co-SLAM substrate with an MLP-based 4D deformation field and a semantic distance-field loss that exploits inter-category boundary information to guide optimisation. It reports improved tracking and reconstruction over both rigid neural-SLAM baselines and prior deformable methods. The presented work builds upon DDS-SLAM and investigates its application to the CRCD, integrating foundation-model-based segmentation with deformable neural SLAM for surgical scene understanding.
\section{Dataset}
The Comprehensive Robotic Cholecystectomy Dataset (CRCD)\cite{Oh2024} comprises cholecystectomy procedures on pig livers using a first-generation daVinci surgical system controlled through the daVinci Research Kit (dVRK). The dataset records two of the three Patient Side Manipulators (PSMs) and the Endoscope Camera Manipulator (ECM). Seven surgeons (A--G) each performed the procedure between one and three times, with 18 episodes being recorded in total.

The dataset spans approximately 75 GB across 164 synchronised Parquet files, covering the following:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stereo endoscopic video} at $1280 \times 720$ resolution (AVC1/MP4) with embedded ROS timestamps. The nominal frame rate is 60\,fps, although episodes A\_1, E\_1, E\_2, and E\_3 were recorded at $\sim$54.55\,Hz.
    \item \textbf{Full robot kinematics} comprising PSM1/2 and MTMR/L joint states, Cartesian poses, gripper angles, and the ECM pose recorded as a 7 dimension vector $(t_x, t_y, t_z, q_x, q_y, q_z, q_w)$, providing the ground truth camera trajectory used in SLAM evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Binary pedal signals} (camera, clutch, monopolar, bipolar) interpolated to align with the kinematic sampling rate.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Velocity Detection}
\label{sec:motion_analysis}
For each frame, the Endoscope Camera Manipulator (ECM) pose was extracted as a 7-vector $(t_x, t_y, t_z, q_x, q_y, q_z, q_w)$ from the synchronised kinematic stream. Linear velocity was computed as the Euclidean norm of the finite-difference position delta, normalised by the inter-frame time interval. Angular velocity was derived from the quaternion inner product between consecutive frames:

\begin{equation}
    \omega_i = \frac{2\arccos\left(|\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{q}_{i+1}|\right)}{\Delta t_i}
\end{equation}

A frame was classified as containing camera motion if either the linear velocity exceeded 0.1\,mm/s or the angular velocity exceeded
0.1\,$^\circ$/s. These thresholds were chosen empirically to exclude sensor noise while capturing deliberate repositioning. Contiguous motion frames were grouped into segments, discarding bursts shorter than 5 frames to filter residual noise. Due to the small size of motion sequences in CRCD, padding was added to extend snippets to a suitable length, or to allow multiple motion sequences to be concatenated. This allowed for a large range of sequences varying from 200 frames to just over 2000 frames in length.

\subsubsection{Scene Motion Classification}
Each segment was further classified by the type of motion present. Tool motion was detected by applying the same finite-difference velocity computation to the PSM1 and PSM2 setpoint Cartesian poses, with a threshold of 1.0\,mm/s (higher than the camera threshold to account for the larger expected tool displacements). Each segment was labelled as \texttt{camera\_only}, \texttt{tool\_only}, or \texttt{tool\_and\_camera}. Finally, tissue motion was classified by manually reviewing the segments. Scripting was then used to extract all segments suitable for SLAM pipelines.

\subsubsection{Selected Sequences}
Finally, sequences were selected to give a varied dataset, with both short and long sequences, as well as varying amounts of motion and deformation. This provides a well balanced challenging surgical SLAM dataset, which is reflective of a real surgical environment. Of these, CholecInstanceSeg is tool-instance only on its $\sim$30{,}000-frame CholecT50-full bulk; its anatomy labels are inherited from the CholecSeg8k base, so for tool-plus-tissue segmentation it effectively collapses to CholecSeg8k. Endoscapes2023 is phase-restricted and excludes the gallbladder-from-liver-bed separation (a major deforming event in the procedure), releasing only 493 public segmentation masks. HeiSurF carries the richest taxonomy (21 full-scene classes) but samples sparsely at two-minute intervals. CholecSeg8k and CRCD are therefore the two datasets that combine dense tool-and-tissue segmentation with full-procedure coverage; CholecSeg8k, however, is limited to 80-frame clips, too short for full SLAM evaluations (When compared to the 200-2000 frame sequences we provide).

 CholecSeg8k provides manual tissue masks on real in-vivo human cholecystectomy, its limitation is that it is monocular and carries no camera-pose ground truth, so it cannot supervise or evaluate a tracker - Additionally to this, the short sequence length makes the dataset redundent for SLAM. CRCD supplies rectified stereo pairs enabling metric depth recovery, per-frame ECM camera pose and dVRK kinematics giving trajectory ground truth. Its costs are that the tissue masks are SAM3 propagations from sparse human labels which are then fine tuned by a human annotator. Additionally the ex-vivo porcine setting removes respiratory motion present in the in-vivo human datasets. For a deformable SLAM system that requires camera-pose ground truth to evaluate tracking, CRCD is the only one of the five that provides it, making it the only densely semantically labelled cholecystectomy dataset suitable for SLAM evaluation.
\subsection{Semantic Annotations}

The expanded CRCD \cite{Oh2024} provides COCO-format annotations for liver segmentation and instrument keypoint detection. Three recordings: C\_1, E\_3, and F\_3, include human-annotated ground truth segmentation masks at every 120th frame; these serve as the reference for the one-shot segmentation experiments. These sparse annotations are propagated to the remaining 119 frames of each 120-frame \texttt{split} using the temporal tracking capabilities of a Track Anything segmentation model~\cite{Yang2023}. The expanded release additionally includes surgeon experience profiles (number and complexity of prior laparoscopic procedures, hours of robotic training).

This coverage is, however, limited for the purposes of deformable-scene SLAM. Only three of the eighteen episodes (C\_1, E\_3, F\_3) carry any segmentation reference.

We therefore expand this annotation set into a dense, per-frame semantic segmentation dataset of our own. It spans 10 episodes (B\_2, C\_1, C\_2, C\_3, E\_1, E\_3, F\_1, F\_3, G\_2, G\_3) and 20 motion-selected snippets, comprising 14{,}688 annotated left-camera frames, each with its rectified right-camera counterpart. Every frame is labelled under one of 3 classes --- Liver, Gallbladder, and Tool --- are chosen as they can be reliably annotated and relevant to the downstream SLAM task. Masks are produced with a SAM3-in-the-loop pipeline: a small set of human-painted anchor keyframes per snippet is propagated bidirectionally through the clip by the SAM3 video predictor, after which the output is inspected, failure frames are corrected and re-committed as additional anchors, and the clip is re-propagated until per-frame quality is reached. The resulting masks are provided in every data package as the dense \texttt{semantic\_instance/} rasters and COCO \texttt{snippet\_annotations.json} of Section~\ref{sec:data_packs}; the full annotation pipeline is presented in Section~\ref{sec:segmentation}.
\subsubsection{SAM~3}
Segment Anything Model 3 (SAM~3)~\cite{Carion2025} is a large  foundation model for promptable segmentation which extends the prior SAM~\cite{Kirillov2023} and SAM~2~\cite{Ravi2024} models. Where prior SAM models addressed promptable visual segmentation via geometric prompts (points, boxes, masks), SAM~3 introduces \textit{Promptable Concept Segmentation} (PCS): concept prompts defined by noun phrases (e.g., ``tools''). These prompts are processed by the text encoder within the shared Perception Encoder backbone, while geometric prompts are handled by the tracker inherited from SAM~2.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/SAM3_arch_ucl.png}
    \caption{SAM~3 architecture: the split between video and text prompting.}
    \label{fig:SAM3ARCH2}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

SAM~3 consists of a \textit{detector} and \textit{tracker} sharing a Perception Encoder (PE) backbone~\cite{Bolya2025}. The DETR-based detector~\cite{Carion2020} fuses text/exemplar prompt tokens with image features via cross-attention, then decodes learned object queries into binary classification logits and bounding box predictions. A \textit{presence token} decouples recognition from localisation, independently assessing whether a candidate region matches the queried concept; this is important for distinguishing semantically similar structures (e.g., liver vs.\ gallbladder). The tracker inherits the SAM~2 streaming architecture: a memory module stores prior frame information, and memory attention conditions each new frame embedding for temporally consistent mask propagation, including through occlusions.
SAM~3 was trained on 4M unique concept labels (52M masks) plus a synthetic set of 38M phrases (1.4B masks), and achieves 75--80\% of human performance on the SA-Co benchmark ($>$207K concepts)~\cite{Carion2025}. For this project, SAM~3 is deployed frozen, without medical-domain fine-tuning, on CRCD endoscopic videos. Its text prompting capability enables concept-level queries (e.g., ``tools'') without geometric prompts, reducing manual annotation dependence relative to SAM~2.

\subsection{Prompting}
\label{sec:prompting}
\subsubsection{Prompting Strategies}
As SAM~3 was deployed frozen, without any fine-tuning---relying on just the base model---the prompting strategy critically determines segmentation quality; prompt errors compound through temporal propagation. CRCD ground truth masks (every 120 frames for C\_1, E\_3, F\_3) served as the source of geometric prompts for liver and gallbladder segmentations. However, as previously stated, these were insufficient for a full SLAM evaluation pipeline. Annotating was therefore expanded to the full CRCD with prompting strategies as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Bounding box:} axis-aligned box from the ground truth mask.
    \item \textbf{Positive points:} points sampled within the mask region.
    \item \textbf{Positive + negative points:} positive points within the mask and negative points sampled from background regions near the object boundary, disambiguating the target from adjacent tissue.
    \item \textbf{Combined:} bounding box, positive, and negative points together, the most information-rich geometric prompt.
    \item \textbf{Text (SAM~3 only):} noun phrase prompts (e.g., ``tools'', ``liver'', ``gallbladder'') with no geometric guidance, testing fully automated semantic segmentation.
    \item \textbf{Masks:} full human annotated masks.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Annotation Pipeline}
\label{sec:segmentation}
Due to the large number of frames present in the CRCD, manual hand-annotation of all chosen sequences would be infeasible. Instead, a keyframe-anchored, human-in-the-loop (HITL) pipeline was built around SAM~3's video propagation (Figure~\ref{fig:SAM3Mod}), running on a FastAPI/Konva.js annotator.

Selected frames throughout each sequence are assigned as keyframes and hand-annotated in the browser annotator (brush, SAM-click, SAM-box and bucket-fill tools, with per-class erasers). Keyframes were typically placed every 60--120 frames depending on sequence difficulty, with additional keyframes provided where significant deformation or camera motion occurred, or when new artifacts entered the scene. For example, CRCD sequence C\_1/001 was provided with keyframes at frames 0, 119, 239 and 359.

Committed anchor masks seed SAM~3's video predictor, which is propagated both forwards and backwards from every anchor --- a slight modification of the as-built SAM~3 pipeline, which propagates forward-only from a single prompt frame. Where the forward and backward sweeps disagree on a frame, the mask retained is the one with the higher SAM~3 confidence score. A drop in confidence, or a drastic and sudden change in the pixel count belonging to a taxonomy class (suggestive of a mask dropout, e.g.\ a tool no longer being registered), flags the frame for review. Because SAM~3's decoder reconstructs (and smooths) masks even at prompt frames, the user-painted anchor masks are re-imposed verbatim at their keyframes after propagation, guaranteeing the human annotation is never silently overwritten. On local hardware, propagation can be deferred entirely --- anchors are painted in a lightweight mode (Without the model loaded) and propagated later on a rented A100 by replaying the same session file.

The propagated masks are rendered as a mask-overlay video which is scrubbed end-to-end by the annotator. 'Failed' frames are corrected in one of three ways, in increasing order of effort: (i) direct hand-fix of the offending frames in the annotator; (ii) insertion of additional keyframes followed by re-propagation of only the affected window (bounding the propagation range to the failure region, then stitching the re-propagated frames back into the accepted sequence); or (iii) full re-anchoring of the sequence. This loop iterates until the annotator accepts the full sequence.

Accepted masks are resolved to a single class per pixel under an occlusion-priority (Tool $>$ Gallbladder $>$ Liver, reflecting physical foreground order; per-frame overrides are supported for sequences where semantic classes overlap), rasterised to per-frame semantic label maps, and passed through a four-gate check (dataset-consistency audit, TUM-loader test, intrinsics sanity check, and byte-stable regeneration). A sequence is only published into the dataset once all four gates pass \emph{and} the human in the loop (HITL) has signed off the final overlay render. \subsubsection{TUM RGB-D conventions}

Image streams are indexed in the TUM manner: \texttt{rgb.txt} and its right-frame counterpart \texttt{rgbright.txt} list one \texttt{timestamp filename} pair per line, with filenames keyed on the ROS timestamp inherited from the synchronised CRCD Parquet files. The ground-truth camera trajectory is written to \texttt{groundtruth.txt} as a sequence of \texttt{timestamp $t_x$ $t_y$ $t_z$ $q_x$ $q_y$ $q_z$ $q_w$} records, expressing the endoscope (ECM-tip) pose as a position and a unit quaternion in a fixed world frame --- the same representation consumed by the standard TUM \texttt{evaluate\_ate} and \texttt{evaluate\_rpe} trajectory-error tooling. Because every stream derives from the source's synchronised timeline, \texttt{associations.txt} records the nearest-timestamp correspondence between the RGB, depth, and pose entries that TUM-style loaders expect.

\subsubsection{Replica semantic conventions}

Semantic ground truth follows the Replica semantic-segmentation convention. \texttt{info\_semantic.json} declares the class taxonomy as an ordered list of \texttt{(id, name)} entries --- here the dense three-class scheme \texttt{\{0:\,Liver, 1:\,Gallbladder, 2:\,Tool\}} --- and per-frame labels are stored in \texttt{semantic\_instance/} as single-channel \texttt{uint16} PNGs. The invariant \texttt{pixel\_value $=$ class\_id $+\,1$} holds throughout, with pixel value 0 reserved for background, so a Replica-style reader recovers the class map directly from the raster while background remains unambiguously non-object. The identical masks are additionally provided as COCO polygons in \texttt{snippet\_annotations.json} for consumers that prefer vector annotations; the polygon and raster representations are kept pixel-consistent by construction. The goal of both of these forced conventions is to allow for ease of use when utilising the CRCD for SLAM models.

\subsubsection{Stereo, depth, and intrinsics}

Two adaptations carry the monocular benchmark formats over to the stereo, depth-free surgical setting. First, the right-camera stream (\texttt{rgbright/}, \texttt{rgbright.txt}) is appended so that stereo methods can compute disparity directly, while monocular readers simply ignore it. \texttt{intrinsics.yaml} carries a \texttt{depth\_placeholder: true} sentinel, signalling that metric depth is to be derived from the stereo pair rather than read from disk; the directory is preserved so that loaders expecting a TUM \texttt{depth/} folder do not fault. Camera intrinsics are written to \texttt{intrinsics.yaml} as the post-rectification matrix

\begin{equation}
    K =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        f_x & 0   & c_x \\
        0   & f_y & c_y \\
        0   & 0   & 1
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

together with the stereo baseline and rectification parameters, all derived from the single ECM stereo calibration (\texttt{ECM\_STEREO\_1280x720\_L2R\_calib\_data\_opencv.pkl}) distributed with CRCD. As the same physical camera is used across the episode set, one calibration applies to every snippet.

\subsubsection{MoGe}
As part of the dataset we provide metrically-anchored depth maps generated with MoGe-2~\cite{Wang2025}. Although MoGe-2 nominally predicts metric depth, its scale is unreliable on out-of-domain close-up endoscopic imagery (on our sequences its raw output runs several times, $\sim$5--10$\times$, larger than the true scene scale), so monocular depth alone cannot be trained against at a fixed scale factor. We therefore anchor it to true metric scale using the rectified stereo pairs: semi-global block matching (SGBM)~\cite{Hirschmuller2008} is run at 120-frame intervals (plus the first and last frame of each sequence), and each anchor's scale is estimated as the median ratio between the stereo and monocular depths over jointly-valid pixels. A cross-anchor consistency check then gates the sequence as a whole. The per-frame scale is then linearly interpolated between surviving anchors, smoothing residual scale drift across the sequence while avoiding the geometric step discontinuities that a piecewise-constant scale would introduce at anchor boundaries. \section{Methods}
\subsection{Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)}
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)~\cite{Mildenhall2020} treat the map as a function rather than an explicit mesh or voxel grid. A neural field $F_\theta$ maps a 3-D point $x \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a viewing direction $d$ to a volume density $\sigma$ and a view-dependent colour $c$,
\begin{equation}
    F_\theta(x, d) = \big(\sigma(x),\; c(x, d)\big),
    \qquad \sigma \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0},\; c \in \mathbb{R}^3 .
\end{equation}
The weights $\theta$ \emph{are} the map: reconstruction is the optimisation of $\theta$, and localisation the optimisation of the camera pose that queries $F_\theta$.

An image is formed by casting a ray $r(z) = o + z\,d$ from the camera centre $o$ along a direction $d$, sampling points $z_1 < \dots < z_N$, and compositing their contributions as weighted sums:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{C}(r) = \sum_i w_i\, c_i, \qquad \hat{D}(r) = \sum_i w_i\, z_i .
    \label{eq:nerf-render}
\end{equation}
In NeRF the weights follow the volume-rendering convention below:
\begin{equation}
    w_i = T_i\big(1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}\big),
    \qquad T_i = \exp\!\Big(-\!\sum_{j<i}\sigma_j \delta_j\Big),
    \label{eq:transmittance}
\end{equation}
with $\delta_i = z_{i+1} - z_i$ the inter-sample spacing, so that mass accumulates at the first opaque surface met along the ray. Rendering the map as a differentiable weighted sum, rather than as a hard ray--surface intersection, makes $F_\theta$ optimisable with respect to both the map weights $\theta$ and the querying camera pose; every method in this chapter relies on this differentiability. Co-SLAM (Section~\ref{sec:coslam}) inherits this volume rendering but replaces the density $\sigma$ with a signed-distance field, whose zero level set defines the surface explicitly and supervises directly from depth.

\subsection{Co-SLAM}
\label{sec:coslam}
Co-SLAM~\cite{Wang2023} is the real-time neural-SDF SLAM substrate that DDS-SLAM extends. Its field maps a point $x \in \mathbb{R}^3$ to a truncated signed-distance value $s(x)$ and a view-independent colour $c(x)$, replacing NeRF's density with geometry that can be supervised directly from depth.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/coslam_architecture.pdf}
    \caption{The Co-SLAM neural-SDF pipeline. A query point is jointly encoded by a multiresolution hash grid and a one-blob coordinate encoding \eqref{eq:joint-enc}; a geometry decoder returns the signed distance $s$ and feature $h$ and a colour decoder the radiance $c$ \eqref{eq:decoders}. Volume rendering \eqref{eq:sdf-weights}--\eqref{eq:nerf-render} yields the colour and depth that supervise both tracking (pose against a frozen map) and mapping (field and keyframe poses).}
    \label{fig:coslam_arch}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Joint coordinate--parametric encoding.} Co-SLAM's defining idea is to encode each point two ways at once, trading the speed of a learnable grid against the smoothness of a coordinate code. A multiresolution hash grid~\cite{Muller2022} stores feature vectors at grid vertices across $L$ resolution levels, from which a point trilinearly interpolates and concatenates a parametric feature $\gamma_{\text{hash}}(x)$; a one-blob coordinate encoding~\cite{Muller2019} $\gamma_{\text{ob}}(x)$ supplies a smooth, hole-filling prior tied to the absolute coordinate. The decoder consumes their concatenation,
\begin{equation}
    \gamma(x) = \big[\, \gamma_{\text{ob}}(x),\; \gamma_{\text{hash}}(x) \,\big].
    \label{eq:joint-enc}
\end{equation}
The hash grid alone is fast and high-capacity but leaves unobserved cells free to take arbitrary values (holes, high-frequency noise); the one-blob term regularises these regions, giving coherent completion where data is absent and sharp detail where it is present; this combination makes single-pass online mapping viable.

\paragraph{Decoders.} Two small multilayer perceptrons decode the joint code: a geometry decoder returns the signed distance and a geometry feature, and a colour decoder consumes that feature to return radiance,
\begin{equation}
    \big(s,\, h\big) = g_\theta\big(\gamma(x)\big), \qquad
    c = f_\theta\big(h,\, \gamma_{\text{hash}}(x)\big).
    \label{eq:decoders}
\end{equation}
The geometry feature $h$ couples appearance to geometry; it is also the natural attachment point for the per-point uncertainty head introduced later.

\paragraph{Volume rendering.} The signed distance $s(x)$ measures the distance from $x$ to the nearest surface --- positive in free space, negative behind it, and truncated to $\pm\tau$ away from the zero level set $s = 0$ that defines the surface. In place of NeRF's density-based transmittance weights \eqref{eq:transmittance}, Co-SLAM converts the signed distances to rendering weights by a symmetric bell centred on the zero-crossing and normalised along the ray,
\begin{equation}
    w_i = \frac{1}{W}\,
    \mathrm{S}\!\Big(\tfrac{s_i}{\tau}\Big)\,
    \mathrm{S}\!\Big(-\tfrac{s_i}{\tau}\Big), \qquad
    W = \sum_{j} \mathrm{S}\!\Big(\tfrac{s_j}{\tau}\Big)\,\mathrm{S}\!\Big(-\tfrac{s_j}{\tau}\Big),
    \label{eq:sdf-weights}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{S}$ the logistic sigmoid and $\tau$ the truncation width. These weights drive the same compositing \eqref{eq:nerf-render} to give the rendered colour $\hat{C}(r)$ and depth $\hat{D}(r)$; concentrating mass in a soft neighbourhood of the surface rather than at a hard intersection keeps the map differentiable in both $\theta$ and the querying pose.

\paragraph{Losses.} Rays are supervised by four terms. Colour and depth match the rendered quantities to the observations,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{rgb}} = \sum_r \big\lVert \hat{C}(r) - C(r) \big\rVert^2,
    \qquad
    \mathcal{L}_{d} = \sum_r \big( \hat{D}(r) - D(r) \big)^2,
    \label{eq:coslam-photo}
\end{equation}
while two geometry terms supervise the SDF \emph{directly} from depth, which lets the map converge in a few iterations per frame. For samples near the surface ($|D - z| \le \tau$) the SDF must equal the along-ray signed distance; for free-space samples in front of it ($z < D - \tau$) it is pinned to the truncation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{sdf}} = \sum_{p \in T} \big( s(p) - (D - z) \big)^2,
    \qquad
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{fs}} = \sum_{p \in F} \big( s(p) - \tau \big)^2 .
    \label{eq:coslam-sdf}
\end{equation}
The total objective is their weighted sum,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{rgb}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rgb}}
    + \lambda_{d}\mathcal{L}_{d}
    + \lambda_{\text{sdf}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{sdf}}
    + \lambda_{\text{fs}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{fs}},
    \label{eq:coslam-total}
\end{equation}
with, in the released configuration, $(\lambda_{\text{rgb}},\lambda_{d},\lambda_{\text{sdf}},\lambda_{\text{fs}}) = (5,\,0.1,\,10^3,\,10)$; the geometry terms are weighted far above the photometric ones during mapping, where surface geometry is built.

\paragraph{Tracking and mapping.} Localisation is \emph{frame-to-model}: the pose of an incoming frame is optimised against the frozen field by minimising \eqref{eq:coslam-total} over sampled rays with respect to the six pose parameters alone, initialised by a constant-velocity extrapolation of the previous two poses,
\begin{equation}
    T_i^{\text{init}} = \big(T_{i-1} T_{i-2}^{-1}\big)\, T_{i-1}.
    \label{eq:const-vel}
\end{equation}
Mapping instead fixes recent poses and optimises the field on rays drawn from the current frame and a keyframe database; periodically a joint bundle adjustment refines the field and the stored keyframe poses together, keeping the trajectory globally consistent. This alternation of frame-to-model tracking and keyframe mapping, over a jointly-encoded neural SDF, is the substrate on which the deformation warp (Section~\ref{sec:ddsslam}) and the contributions of this thesis are built.
\subsection{DDS-SLAM}
\label{sec:ddsslam}
DDS-SLAM builds on Co-SLAM with a time-conditioned deformation warp: a small network $D_\phi$ predicts, for a point $x$ at time $t$, a displacement $\Delta x$ carrying it back to a canonical representation --- the initial frame. To capture fine detail, $x$ and $t$ are first projected into a high-dimensional frequency space by a positional encoding~\cite{Mildenhall2020}:
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_{\text{freq}}(p) = \big\langle \sin(2^l\pi p),\, \cos(2^l\pi p) \big\rangle_{l=0}^{M-1},
    \label{eq:posenc}
\end{equation}
applied component-wise, with $M$ the number of frequency bands. The encoded position and time are concatenated and passed through $D_\phi$ to produce the displacement, added to the query point to give its canonical location:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta x = D_\phi\big(\gamma_{\text{freq}}(x),\, \gamma_{\text{freq}}(t)\big),
    \qquad x_{\text{canon}} = x + \Delta x .
    \label{eq:warp}
\end{equation}
The displacement is anchored to zero at the first frame, $\Delta x \equiv 0$ when $t = 0$, fixing the canonical configuration to the initial frame rather than leaving it free; without this anchor the static field and the warp would be jointly unconstrained, able to trade geometry against motion arbitrarily. $x_{\text{canon}}$ is then encoded and decoded exactly as in the static Co-SLAM field of Section~\ref{sec:coslam}, so the SDF geometry is represented \emph{once}, in canonical space, with all temporal variation absorbed by the warp. Crucially, $D_\phi$ is supervised \emph{only} through the rendering loss (there is no direct displacement target), so the field must infer scene motion purely from photometric and geometric consistency across time.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/deformation_warp.pdf}
    \caption{The DDS-SLAM deformation warp. A frequency-encoded network $D_\phi$ predicts, for a query point $x$ at time $t$, a displacement $\Delta x$ mapping it to its location in the canonical (frame-0) configuration \eqref{eq:warp}, where the static SDF is evaluated. Anchored to zero at $t=0$, the warp absorbs all temporal variation so the geometry is represented once, in canonical space.}

\subsection{Uncertainty-Aware Down-Weighting}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
The first contribution of this thesis is transferring the aleatoric-uncertainty principle established for Gaussian splatting in WildGS-SLAM~\cite{Zheng2024} into a NeRF setting, using a learned uncertainty to attenuate unreliable measurements in both mapping and tracking. A lightweight head predicts a per-ray variance $\sigma^2(r)$ from the geometry feature $h$; under a Gaussian observation model, the head is trained by the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the photometric residual~\cite{KendallGal2017}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{r}\frac{1}{2}
    \left(\frac{\|C(r)-\hat{C}(r)\|^2}{\sigma^2(r)} + \log\sigma^2(r)\right)
    \label{eq:nll}
\end{equation}
The $1/\sigma^2$ term rewards small residuals where the model is confident, while the $\log\sigma^2$ term penalises blanket high variance; the head therefore learns to raise $\sigma^2$ where the residual is irreducibly large --- the deforming and independently-moving regions the static field cannot explain.

The learned variance is consumed by both halves of the system. During mapping it enters directly through \eqref{eq:nll}, so high-uncertainty rays contribute proportionally less to the field optimisation. During tracking, where the map is fixed and only the six camera-pose parameters vary, the same variance is converted into a per-ray trust weight that down-weights unreliable rays in the pose solve:
\begin{equation}
    w(r) = \mathrm{clamp}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^2(r)},\; w_{\min},\; w_{\max}\right),
    \label{eq:unc-weight}
\end{equation}
applied multiplicatively to the per-ray colour and depth residuals. The weight is detached from the optimisation graph, so $\sigma^2$ acts purely as an attenuation and does not itself back-propagate into the pose.

Two guards make the weight well-behaved. First, on rays where the map holds no confident surface (accumulated rendering opacity below $0.5$), $\sigma^2$ sits at its clamp floor and \eqref{eq:unc-weight} would assign such rays \emph{maximum} trust; since a ray without a surface carries no reliable pose information, its weight is instead reset to the neutral value $w = 1$. Second, the weight vector is mean-normalised, $w \leftarrow w / \bar{w}$, so that the down-weighting \emph{redistributes} trust across the sampled rays at a fixed total rather than silently rescaling the photometric terms against the geometric ones in \eqref{eq:coslam-total}. A single uncertainty estimate thus serves as both a mapping regulariser and a tracking robustifier, reducing the influence of deforming tissue and moving instruments wherever they would otherwise corrupt the estimate.

\subsection{Motion Attribution}
\label{sec:motion}
The second contribution decides, for every frame, whether the camera is moving and which regions move independently of it, and gates the pose solve accordingly.
\paragraph{Semantic districts.} A frozen self-supervised vision transformer (base DINOv3-B/16, register variant~\cite{Simeoni2025}; the choice of backbone is examined below) produces a patch feature grid; its $\ell_2$-normalised rows are clustered into $K$ districts with k-means. Districts are \emph{semantic} rather than spatial: an instrument votes as one bloc regardless of where it lies in the frame, which lets independent movers self-identify without a mask.

Optical flow is computed with RAFT~\cite{Teed2020} between the current frame and a reference frame $\delta$ frames earlier ($\delta = 8$), integrating motion well above the flow noise floor on these slow-moving sequences. Each valid district $k$ then casts a vote consisting of its median flow $f_k$, its median depth $Z_k$, and its centroid $p_k$; the median makes each vote robust to its own outlier pixels. A single tiny egomotion model is fit across the district votes, with regressors chosen so that each motion type carries a distinct depth signature:
\begin{equation}
    f_k \;\approx\; u \;+\; v\,\zeta_k \;+\; d\,r_k\,\zeta_k,
    \qquad \zeta_k = \frac{Z_{\text{med}}}{Z_k},
    \quad r_k = \frac{p_k - p_0}{r_{\text{norm}}}
    \label{eq:vote-fit}
\end{equation}
where $p_0$ is the image principal point and $r_{\text{norm}}$ a normalising radius. Here $u$ is depth blind i.e., camera rotation shifts every district equally, $v$ a depth-scaled slide (lateral translation moves near districts more, as $1/Z$), and $d$ a depth-scaled radial zoom (forward motion expands the field). Because $\zeta_k$ is a ratio of depths it is dimensionless, so the unknown global scale of the monocular depth cancels.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/depth_signature.pdf}
    \caption{The depth signature that makes the egomotion fit \eqref{eq:vote-fit} identifiable. A camera turn (rotation) shifts near and far districts equally; a lateral slide moves near districts more, as $1/Z$; forward motion expands the field radially. Fitting the three simultaneously separates the motion types and cancels the unknown global depth scale.}
    \label{fig:depth_signature}
\end{figure}

The model is fit by least squares and made robust by a median-absolute-deviation reweighting: districts that fit no camera motion (tools and deforming tissue) fall out as outliers and receive a low trust
\begin{equation}
    w_k = \frac{1}{1 + \big(\max(0,\rho_k - \mathrm{med})/\mathrm{scale}\big)^2},
    \qquad \rho_k = \|f_k - \hat{f}_k\|.
    \label{eq:vote-trust}
\end{equation}
Two statistics of the votes decide whether the camera is still: a minority test $q_{10}$, which looks at the tenth-percentile district flow magnitude, and a majority test $\mathrm{dis3}$, the fraction of districts inconsistent in the Sampson (epipolar) sense~\cite{Hartley2004} with a single fundamental matrix $F$ fit to the dense flow:
\begin{equation}
    q_{10} = P_{10}\big(\{\|f_k\|\}\big), \qquad
    \mathrm{dis3} = \frac{|\{k : \mathrm{Sampson}(F,k) > 3\,\text{px}\}|}{|\{\text{valid }k\}|}
    \label{eq:vote-stats}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \textbf{freeze} \iff q_{10} < \tau_q \;\;\textbf{or}\;\; \mathrm{dis3} > \tau_d
    \qquad (\tau_q = 2.5\,\text{px},\; \tau_d = 0.5)
    \label{eq:cprime}
\end{equation}
The minority test encodes that stillness requires the quietest districts to agree the world is still, so a single large mover against a quiet background does not imply the camera moved; the majority test asks whether any one rigid-camera story explains the motion. They are combined disjunctively because requiring both simultaneously restores precision on one motion regime only by destroying recall on the other.

\paragraph{Freeze mechanics.} When \eqref{eq:cprime} reports the camera still, the pose is copied from the previous frame, the solve is skipped, and the frame's relative pose against its anchoring keyframe is refreshed so the bookkeeping stays consistent; mapping still runs. Keyframe poses anchor the non-keyframes between them, and the global bundle adjustment re-optimises every keyframe on every cycle, so an unprotected frozen keyframe would drift and silently carry its whole block of anchored frames with it, undoing the freeze after the fact. Gate-frozen keyframes are therefore re-pinned to their frozen pose after every bundle-adjustment cycle. This re-pin prevents the pose from jumping massively after each bundle-adjustment cycle; as otherwise BA would attempt to re-optimise a frozen pose; thus moving it.

\paragraph{Threshold calibration.} The thresholds in \eqref{eq:cprime} are calibrated offline rather than tuned in the loop. The gate logs its per-frame statistics $(q_{10}, \mathrm{dis3})$ during a run; sweeping these logs against the ground-truth camera motion yields the precision and recall of every candidate operating point $(\tau_q, \tau_d)$. One discretisation detail matters in practice: with $K = 12$ districts, $\mathrm{dis3}$ is quantised to twelfths, so a strict inequality at $\tau_d = 0.5$ silently excludes frames on which exactly half the districts dissent; candidate thresholds must be placed between attainable values of the statistic, not on them.

\paragraph{Featuriser choice.} The districts are built from a frozen self-supervised backbone. This was measured two ways: how well a backbone separates an independently-moving region in feature space, and whether that separation actually changes the freeze decision. For the first, a probe-margin test scored each backbone by the mean cosine similarity of a probe patch on the mover to the rest of the moving region, minus its similarity to everything else. DINOv2-S/14 (register variant)~\cite{Oquab2023} scored highest at $+0.33$, base DINOv3-B/16~\cite{Simeoni2025} $+0.29$, and a DINOv3 backbone fine-tuned for surgical segmentation only $+0.07$ at its final layer. The fine-tuned collapse is expected - a segmentation objective pulls all features of a class together, which removes the intra-tissue variation the clustering relies on. The first result is therefore that the featuriser should not be fine-tuned; a raw backbone is creating a better set of features than one fine tuned, images of the features and their cosine similarities are shown in...

However, running both raw backbones end-to-end on the evaluation sequences, the freeze decision is near-identical regardless of backbone: $q_{10}$ correlates at $0.99$ between DINOv2-S/14 and base DINOv3-B/16, $\mathrm{dis3}$ is effectively the same, and $94$--$95\%$ of the freeze decisions agree. The $5$--$6\%$ of decisions that do differ are a result of the base DINOv3 model performing slightly above DINOv2, with higher freeze precision and recall on both evaluation sequences, and a small tracking improvement on the deforming sequence.

\paragraph{Per-ray trust variants (ablated).} The same votes support a softer consumer: the fit residuals of \eqref{eq:vote-trust}, or a depth-normalised deviation from the district consensus, can be painted into a dense per-ray motion trust and applied in the pose solve multiplicatively with the uncertainty weight of \eqref{eq:unc-weight} on tracked frames. These variants are evaluated in the ablations and not retained in the locked configuration, for measured reasons. Behavioural audits against kinematic ground truth showed the depth-pooled variant responds to camera rotation rather than to scene motion: its normalisation is exact for camera-translation parallax, which scales as $1/Z$, but mis-scales rotational flow, which is depth-independent. The sharper de-rotated variant discriminates scene motion nearly perfectly on the design sequences yet degrades held-out sequences, its per-frame muting noise compounding over long trajectories. And stacking any trust behind the freeze gate starves the solve: the gate has already removed the confidently-still frames, so the trust mutes precisely the frames that still need tracking. The freeze decision --- which removes ambiguous frames outright rather than re-weighting within them --- is the consumer that survives measurement.

\paragraph{Zero-motion prior.} The freeze is a hard, per-frame decision, so a softer mechanism is used alongside it to handle the drift on frames that are tracked rather than frozen. On near-static sequences the pose solve still over-travels on frames the gate does not freeze - either frames that are still but sit just below the threshold, or individual pose axes that the narrow endoscopic geometry leaves weakly observable. To address this, a zero-motion prior penalises per-frame pose change directly, adding
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{zm}} = \lambda_r \lVert \Delta\mathbf{r} \rVert^2 + \lambda_t \lVert \Delta\mathbf{t} \rVert^2
    \label{eq:zeromotion}
\end{equation}
to the tracking loss, where $\Delta\mathbf{r}$ and $\Delta\mathbf{t}$ are the rotation and translation of the current pose relative to the previous committed pose. The penalty is the same in every direction, but its effect is not. The SDF data term has its own per-axis curvature (approximately the squared flow Jacobian), so an observable axis with a steep gradient overrules the prior and keeps the real motion, while a flat, unobservable axis is pinned towards zero. The prior is added before the best-iterate selection so that it binds, and $\lambda_r$ and $\lambda_t$ are calibrated against the SDF loss scale. This differs from simply lowering the dissent threshold, which only recovers still-frame recall by mis-freezing the deforming sequence; the prior instead leaves the deforming case alone, since there the data gradients are strong enough to overrule it, and so regularises the near-static regime without a penalty on the moving one.

\subsection{Optimiser-Induced Pose Drift in Bundle Adjustment}
\label{sec:ba_floor}

\paragraph{The mechanism.} The global bundle adjustment of Section~\ref{sec:coslam} re-optimises every keyframe pose on every cycle (one cycle per mapped frame, every $m = 5$ frames) with a freshly constructed Adam optimiser~\cite{Kingma2015}. Adam's update normalises each gradient coordinate by a running estimate of its own magnitude,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\theta \;=\; -\,\eta \,\frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon},
    \qquad
    \big|\Delta\theta\big| \;\approx\; \eta \quad \text{at the first steps of a fresh optimiser,}
    \label{eq:adam-floor}
\end{equation}
since bias correction makes $\hat{m}/\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ a unit-magnitude, sign-like quantity whenever the gradient is non-zero --- \emph{regardless of how small that gradient is}. A freshly constructed optimiser has no accumulated second-moment history to shrink this ratio, so each cycle moves every pose coordinate by approximately the learning rate. With $S$ pose steps per cycle and learning rate $\eta$, every keyframe translation walks by up to $\eta\, S \sqrt{3}$ per cycle whether the residual is a genuine misalignment or pure sampling noise; and at the released batch size the gradient is essentially always noise, since the global sample is spread across all keyframes, leaving of order thirty rays per keyframe per iteration. At the released settings ($\eta = 10^{-4}$, $S = 4$) the predicted floor is ${\sim}0.35$\,mm per cycle.

\paragraph{The evidence.} The predicted floor is observed directly: the mean per-cycle keyframe displacement measures $0.31$--$0.40$\,mm in every configuration tested, including the unmodified baseline, and is insensitive to the scene, the sequence, and every measurement-side robustification. That insensitivity is itself diagnostic: because Adam normalises the loss scale away, re-weighting the residuals --- including the uncertainty weighting of Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty} extended into the bundle-adjustment loss --- changes the \emph{direction} of the pull but cannot change its \emph{size}. The walk surfaces in the trajectory as a characteristic fingerprint: estimated steps landing on keyframe indices run $3.4$--$4.3\times$ larger than steps between interior frames, in every arm and every sequence, because interior frames are anchored to their keyframe's gauge while the accumulated keyframe drift is absorbed at block boundaries. A constant-velocity initialisation of the next tracking solve then extrapolates each cycle's displacement forward, amplifying the seam.

\paragraph{The correction.} Both entry points are closed with configuration, not architecture: the bundle-adjustment pose learning rate is reduced an order of magnitude ($\eta = 10^{-5}$), dropping the floor tenfold while leaving genuinely observable misalignments --- which survive many cycles of consistent gradient --- free to converge; and the constant-velocity initialisation is disabled, since ground-truth inter-frame motion on these sequences ($0.16$--$0.33$\,mm) sits far inside the tracker's per-frame reach, so the extrapolation carries more optimiser artefact than motion signal. The correction was pre-registered and confirmed at its predicted magnitude: the per-cycle keyframe walk fell from ${\sim}0.35$\,mm to $0.043$\,mm (predicted ${\sim}0.04$), the keyframe-boundary step ratio fell from $3.4$--$4.3\times$ to $1.5$--$1.8\times$, estimated path length on the stillest design sequence halved, and rendering quality was unchanged --- confirming the removed motion was optimiser artefact, not signal.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval_metrics}

Evaluation covers the two halves of a SLAM system --- \emph{tracking} (camera-pose accuracy) and \emph{mapping} (reconstruction quality, judged through rendering) --- plus behavioural diagnostics designed for the specific failure modes of monocular surgical sequences, and segmentation accuracy for the semantic components. Quantitative results in this work-in-progress are currently reported at a single random seed ($n{=}1$) as screening runs; the target protocol reports the mean over $n{=}3$ random seeds, with the baseline's seed-to-seed standard deviation defining the noise floor that an improvement must clear before it is claimed.

\subsubsection{Tracking: Sim(3) Absolute Trajectory Error}
\label{sec:eval_ate}

The primary tracking metric is the Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) after a \emph{similarity} (Sim(3)) alignment. Given estimated camera centres $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ and ground-truth centres $\{\mathbf{y}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$, paired one-to-one by frame index, the Umeyama closed-form solution \cite{umeyama1991} recovers the scale $s$, rotation $R$ and translation $\mathbf{t}$ minimising
\begin{equation}
(s^\ast, R^\ast, \mathbf{t}^\ast) \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{s,R,\mathbf{t}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl\lVert \mathbf{y}_i - \left( sR\,\mathbf{x}_i + \mathbf{t} \right) \bigr\rVert^2 ,
  \label{eq:umeyama}
\end{equation}
via the SVD of the centred cross-covariance $H = \sum_i (\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{y}_i - \bar{\mathbf{y}})^{\!\top} = U S V^{\!\top}$, with $R^\ast = V \operatorname{diag}(1,1,\det(VU^{\!\top}))\, U^{\!\top}$ and the scale $s^\ast$ given by the ratio of the (sign-corrected) singular-value sum to the estimate's centred variance. The per-frame error and its summary are then
\begin{equation}
e_i = \bigl\lVert \mathbf{y}_i - \left( s^\ast R^\ast \mathbf{x}_i + \mathbf{t}^\ast \right)\bigr\rVert, \qquad \mathrm{ATE}_{\mathrm{RMSE}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_i e_i^2},
\end{equation}
reported in millimetres alongside the mean, median and maximum.

\subsubsection{Tracking: scale-free shape diagnostics}
\label{sec:eval_shape}

CRCD ground-truth camera motion is small (path lengths of ${\sim}20$--$80$\,mm, i.e.\ ${\sim}0.05$--$0.4$\,mm/frame), placing it near or below the tracker's noise floor (\emph{sub-SNR}). In this regime a small ATE can be achieved by a trajectory that is merely \emph{compact} rather than \emph{correct}, so ATE alone is insufficient and two complementary, scale-free diagnostics are always reported with it:

\paragraph{Path-length ratio.} With $L(\mathbf{z}) = \sum_i \lVert \mathbf{z}_{i+1} - \mathbf{z}_i \rVert$ the trajectory arc length,
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\mathrm{path}} \;=\; \frac{s^\ast \, L(\mathbf{x})}{L(\mathbf{y})}
\end{equation}
measures over- or under-travel after scale correction. $\rho_{\mathrm{path}} > 1$ indicates the estimate accumulates excess motion (jitter or exaggerated steps); $\rho_{\mathrm{path}} \to 0$ exposes a degenerate ``frozen'' trajectory that ATE alone would reward on near-static sequences.

\paragraph{Aligned per-axis correlation.} The absolute Pearson correlation $|r|$ between the \emph{Sim(3)-aligned} estimate and the ground truth along the dominant motion axis (the axis of largest ground-truth extent), together with the mean over all three axes. Correlation is invariant to affine rescaling, so this isolates \emph{shape} tracking from any residual scale artefact. The alignment step is essential: because Sim(3) alignment includes a rotation, correlating the \emph{raw} estimate against the ground truth compares physically different axes and can read near zero even when the aligned trajectory tracks well; this artefact was observed and corrected during this work.

\subsubsection{Rendering: PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS}
\label{sec:eval_render}

Mapping quality is judged by re-rendering every training view from the final model and comparing against the input frames, using the standard triplet.

\paragraph{PSNR} (peak signal-to-noise ratio), on images in $[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PSNR} = 20 \log_{10}\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}} \right), \qquad \mathrm{MSE} = \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\textstyle\sum_{p \in \Omega} \bigl( I(p) - \hat{I}(p) \bigr)^2 ,
\end{equation}
a purely pixel-wise fidelity measure (higher is better).

\paragraph{SSIM} (structural similarity \cite{Wang2004}) compares local luminance, contrast and structure statistics computed under an $11{\times}11$ Gaussian window ($\sigma{=}1.5$):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SSIM}(p) = \frac{(2\mu_1\mu_2 + C_1)(2\sigma_{12} + C_2)} {(\mu_1^2 + \mu_2^2 + C_1)(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + C_2)},
\end{equation}
with $C_1 = 0.01^2$, $C_2 = 0.03^2$, averaged over the image (higher is better). SSIM is less sensitive than PSNR to uniform brightness shifts and more sensitive to structural degradation (blur, ghosting).

\paragraph{LPIPS} (learned perceptual image patch similarity \cite{zhang2018lpips}) embeds both images with a fixed pre-trained CNN and measures the weighted $\ell_2$ distance between normalised deep features across layers $l$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{LPIPS}(I, \hat{I}) = \sum_l \frac{1}{H_l W_l} \sum_{h,w} \bigl\lVert w_l \odot \bigl( \phi_l(I)_{hw} - \phi_l(\hat{I})_{hw} \bigr) \bigr\rVert_2^2
\end{equation}
(lower is better). LPIPS correlates with human judgement where PSNR/SSIM fail: a render can be blurry-but-average (good PSNR, poor LPIPS) or sharp with small misalignments (poor PSNR, good LPIPS); reporting all three disambiguates which regime a change lives in.

On Semantic-SuPer sequences the supplied ``ground-truth'' poses are placeholder identities, so tracking metrics are meaningless there and rendering metrics are the sole arbiter; conversely CRCD provides kinematic camera poses, so both halves are evaluated.

\subsubsection{Depth-$L_1$ (self-consistency)}
\label{sec:eval_depth}

CRCD ships no measured ground-truth depth: the depth input is itself generated (MoGe-2). The Depth-$L_1$ metric is therefore defined as \emph{input-versus-output, per model}: the mean absolute difference (in mm) between the depth a model \emph{renders} and the depth that same model was \emph{given} on that frame,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Depth}\text{-}L_1 = \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{V}} \bigl| \hat{D}(p) - c\,D_{\mathrm{in}}(p) \bigr| ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{V}$ masks invalid pixels ($\leq 0$ or outside a plausible metric range in either map) and $c$ is the model's own depth pre-scaling factor, so both maps live in the frame the model actually consumed. This is explicitly a \emph{self-consistency} measure (how faithfully the reconstruction absorbed its geometric supervision), not an accuracy-versus-reality measure, and it is stated as such wherever quoted.

\subsubsection{Segmentation: mean Intersection-over-Union}
\label{sec:eval_miou}

Semantic components (the DINO-based segmentation heads used by the benchmarked semantic-SLAM baselines) are evaluated by class-wise Intersection-over-Union over the four CRCD classes $\{$background, liver, gallbladder, tool$\}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IoU}_c = \frac{|P_c \cap G_c|}{|P_c \cup G_c|}, \qquad \mathrm{mIoU} = \tfrac{1}{4} \textstyle\sum_c \mathrm{IoU}_c ,
\end{equation}
accumulated over all pixels of the evaluation set. Generalisation is judged under a leave-one-snippet-out protocol: for each benchmark snippet the head is trained on the remaining snippets (with two training snippets held out as an inner validation set for model selection, avoiding the leakage of a random frame-level split), and tested on the never-seen snippet. For the four single-snippet episodes this equals leave-one-\emph{episode}-out and yields a true cross-patient number; the one snippet sharing an episode with training data is in-domain under this protocol and is reported separately from the cross-episode headline mean.

\subsubsection{Data preparation}

Sequences are consumed through a staging pipeline executed once per sequence and cached, so that every method variant trains on byte-identical inputs. Frames are rectified with the published ECM stereo calibration (Section~\ref{sec:data_packs}); per-frame depth is generated with MoGe-2 on the rectified left frames and anchored to metric scale by semi-global stereo matching on the rectified pairs at 120-frame intervals, with a consistency gate rejecting sequences whose anchors disagree (I.e., where the scaling changes significantly between intervals.); scene bounds are derived from the resulting metric depth; and the published semantic rasters are rectified alongside the imagery, providing the four-class map (background, liver, gallbladder, instrument) consumed by the semantic and instrument-masking components.

\subsubsection{Implementation}

All experiments run a single code base forked from the public DDS-SLAM release~\cite{Shan2024}, with every modification gated behind a configuration flag that defaults to \emph{off}. A bit-identical regression gate (identical random-number state after model construction, identical parameter counts, identical \texttt{state\_dict} keys against the upstream commit) guarantees that the unmodified configuration reproduces the released implementation exactly, so the baseline serves as a provably untouched control and every measured difference is attributable to exactly one enabled component. Experiments execute on Google Colab GPUs (Tesla T4, 15\,GB; A100, 40\,GB for the largest configurations) under PyTorch~2.x / CUDA~12.x, with the tiny-cuda-nn hash-encoding backend rebuilt for the resident GPU architecture. TF32 tensor-core arithmetic is disabled throughout: the SDF and pose optimisation are precision-sensitive, and TF32 introduced run-to-run nondeterminism on A100-class hardware. Comparisons in this work-in-progress are reported at a single seed ($n{=}1$) as screening-grade results; the target protocol reports the mean over three seeds ($n{=}3$) with the baseline seed spread as the noise floor, and single-seed numbers are re-run at $n{=}3$ before any final claim is drawn. Every run emits a fixed artifact set (rendered frames, the estimated trajectory, per-frame diagnostics, and a composite diagnostic video), and all evaluation is performed by a common tool chain (Section~\ref{sec:eval_metrics}) applied identically to every arm.

The main observations:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The model generalises.} BDDS-SLAM improves tracking on all five sequences, and the largest gain is on a sequence it never saw during development ($-51\%$ on C\_2/001; $-19\%$ and $-39\%$ on the other two held-out sequences). The held-out set is $54$--$82\%$ camera-still, which is exactly the regime the freeze, its bundle-adjustment re-pin, and the corrected pose step were built for - the mechanism and the result agree.
\item \textbf{Long sequences no longer degrade.} G\_3/001 (1987 frames) is the best result of the five ($2.15$\,mm, $|r| = 0.976$, PSNR $28.8$), and C\_3/001 (1527 frames) improves despite its length. Sequence length was the failure mode of the soft per-frame weighting variants (Section~\ref{sec:results_ablations}); the freeze architecture removes it, since a frozen frame accumulates no drift at all.
\item \textbf{Trajectory shape improves with trajectory error.} The aligned dominant-axis correlation on the held-out sequences reaches $0.95$--$0.98$ (C\_2, G\_3) against $0.86$--$0.93$ for the base, and the over-travel path ratio on C\_2/001 falls from $6.3$ to $1.9$: the estimate is not just closer to the true path, it has largely stopped oscillating around it.
\item \textbf{Rendering is preserved, not traded.} Across the five sequences the rendering battery stays level with the base (mean PSNR $24.9$, LPIPS $0.42$): the tracking gains do not come at reconstruction cost, consistent with the design in which the map continues training on frozen frames.
\item \textbf{The deforming sequence remains the hardest.} E\_3/005 improves ($-18\%$) but keeps the weakest shape correlation ($0.46$), and its single-seed spread is the widest we observe. The gate's remaining error there is concentrated in a segment where the true camera motion sits below the optical-flow noise floor, which no threshold on a flow statistic can recover - the honest limit of the approach.
\end{itemize}

Single-seed caveat: per the protocol above, these are screening-grade numbers; the three-seed re-run of the final configuration is the confirmation step, with the base seed spread ($\pm 0.76$\,mm on E\_3/005) as the noise floor.

Three points the ladder makes. First, the gains are separable and attributable: the freeze pays on the deforming sequence, the corrected bundle-adjustment step pays on the still one, and their combination transfers to the held-out set (Table~\ref{tab:results_main}). \section{Benchmarking against SoTA Semantic-SLAM pipelines}
\label{sec:benchmarking}
To adequately measure the performance of BDDS-SLAM against the state of the art (SoTA), a range of models was selected: Semantic-SuPer, SNI-SLAM, SGS-SLAM and SemGauss-SLAM, together forming the current benchmark for semantic SLAM pipelines. These models were all evaluated on the same CRCD sequences as BDDS-SLAM, using the models as provided by the relevant authors; configuration changes were limited to input paths, camera intrinsics, and class taxonomy, so that no method was re-tuned. Each method consumes the same rectified RGB stream; depth supervision, where a method requires it, is the same stereo-anchored MoGe-2 depth. Metrics follow Section~\ref{sec:eval_metrics}.
